Technology · frame to console

Seven stages between the pavement and the screen.

Every frame makes the same journey: out of a camera the lot already has, through a detector, against a calibrated map, into a vote, onto a screen — in under a second, on hardware you control. This page walks the whole pipeline, in the order a frame travels.

The pipeline

Seven stages, one direction.

No stage is exotic on its own. The discipline is the order: nothing reaches the console that hasn't been detected, matched against the map, and confirmed across frames.

ingest→ decode→ detect→ match→ smooth→ record→ publish
/01

Ingest

Sightline connects to any camera that speaks RTSP — the protocol nearly every IP camera already speaks. No agent on the camera, no firmware to touch. Streams drop in the real world; when one does, the camera manager backs off and reconnects on its own.

rtsp · auto-reconnect
/02

Decode

Frames come off the stream at five per second — a rate chosen on purpose. Parking happens at parking speed: five looks a second misses nothing that matters, and the pipeline stays light enough for ordinary hardware.

5 fps
/03

Detect

Each frame runs through a YOLOv8 oriented-bounding-box model. Upright rectangles spill into the next stall the moment parking is angled; oriented boxes rotate with the vehicle, so each car resolves to the space it actually sits in.

yolov8-obb
/04

Match

Every space is a named polygon drawn once during calibration. Each detection is clipped against each outline and the overlap measured — intersection over union, not a center-point guess. A car counts only when it genuinely sits inside the space.

polygon · iou
/05

Smooth

Raw per-frame results jitter: shadows shift, a van blocks the view, cars cruise the aisle. Each space holds a vote across its recent frames, with separate thresholds for becoming occupied and becoming free. What leaves this stage is a decision, not a flicker.

frame votes
/06

Record

Every confirmed change is written to Postgres as a timestamped event, and analytics are computed from those intervals time-weighted: a space filled for three hours counts for three hours, however many detections fired along the way.

postgres · time-weighted
/07

Publish

Confirmed state pushes to the console over WebSocket — no polling, no page refresh. The space grid, the totals, and the annotated view move together. From a car pulling in to the screen changing: under a second.

websocket · <1 s
Calibration

Drawn once, to the paint.

Per-space truth starts with a map. During setup, each stall is traced as a polygon over the camera's own view — to the painted lines, not to a grid — and given a name. From then on, every detection is measured against those outlines, so the system reports "space A-14 is taken," not "something is somewhere."

The map is done once and survives until the paint changes. On Lot A, all 100 polygons sit under a single camera view.

Where it runs

The footage never leaves the building.

On-premises isn't a deployment option here — it's the architecture. Ingest, decode, detection, matching, and history all run on hardware you control, inside your network. There is no cloud leg in the pipeline for video to travel.

Stays on your hardware

  • The camera stream and every decoded frame
  • The detection model and its weights
  • The calibrated space map
  • The Postgres event history

Reaches a browser

  • Space states and counts
  • Timestamps of confirmed changes
  • Analytics computed from them

state, not video — over a websocket, to the console you open.

The boundary

What Sightline does not do.

A camera system earns trust by what it refuses to be. Sightline answers one question — is this space taken or open — and is built so it cannot answer others.

no

No plate reading

There is no ANPR in the system. Sightline has no component that reads, stores, or matches license plates — it cannot tell you whose car is in a space, only that the space is taken.

no

No person identification

The model detects vehicles in stalls. It does not identify, track, or profile people — a pedestrian crossing the lot is not a detection, and there is nothing downstream to identify them with.

no

No video retention

What history keeps is a log of occupancy changes — useful for planning and audits, useless for tracking anyone. The footage itself stays on your hardware and is not what Sightline stores.

The stack

Boring on purpose.

Every layer is established, inspectable technology your own team can reason about — no exotic runtime, no proprietary black box. The entire Lot A pipeline, camera to console, runs on a single ordinary machine.
