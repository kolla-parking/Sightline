Why occupancy history should be measured in seconds, not events

Monish Munagala 2026-08-28 6 min read

At nine in the morning, a commuter pulls into your lot, parks, and walks away. At five in the evening she comes back and leaves. In most parking analytics, that car is one event — a single tick in the morning column of a bar chart. Meanwhile, a space near the entrance turns over eight times in the same day: eight events, eight ticks, eight times the apparent activity. Yet it is the commuter who held a piece of your asset for eight solid hours.

Count events, and the car that used your lot the most becomes nearly invisible. That inversion sits quietly under a lot of parking dashboards, and it is worth pulling into the light.

/01What event counting actually measures

An event-counted history answers the question how often did things change? — which is a measure of churn, not of fullness. A lot that fills at 8 a.m. and stays full until 6 p.m. produces almost no events all day; on a chart of state changes it looks dead precisely while it is straining at capacity. A half-empty lot with restless short-stay traffic produces events constantly and looks busy while seats sit open.

But the questions an operator actually asks are questions about time. How full were we at 2 p.m.? Which hour is our real peak? Do we have room to sell more monthly permits? None of these are answered by counting changes. They are answered by knowing, for every second of the day, how many spaces were occupied.

"How full were we at 2 p.m.?" is a question about time. Answering it with a count of events is answering a different question.

/02The honest unit is the space-second

A parking lot's capacity is not really a number of spaces — it is spaces multiplied by time. A 100-space lot offers 360,000 space-seconds every hour. Occupancy for that hour is simply the occupied seconds divided by the total: fill 68 spaces for the whole hour and you used 244,800 of them, 68 percent, whether one car sat all hour or twelve took turns. The unit absorbs churn and dwell alike, which is exactly what a truthful average must do.

Picture two lots side by side. In the first, a hundred commuters arrive before nine and stay past five: two events per space per day, and a lot that runs effectively full for eight hours. In the second, short-stay visitors come and go all day, and the lot never climbs past half full. Counted in events, the second lot looks several times busier than the first. Counted in space-seconds, the ranking flips — and it is the second ranking that matches what the owner's revenue, the attendants' experience, and the drivers circling for a spot all already know.

/03How Sightline computes it

Sightline watches every space as its own polygon, so each space's day is a clean series of intervals: occupied from 8:40 to 11:15, open until 11:32, occupied again until 2:06. To build hourly history, the system splits each interval wherever it crosses an hour boundary and sums the seconds that land in each bucket. The 8:40-to-11:15 car contributes 1,200 seconds to the eight o'clock hour, 3,600 to nine, 3,600 to ten, and 900 to eleven. Nothing is rounded to the nearest event, and nothing is lost when a car outlasts the chart's resolution.

The boundary split is the detail that keeps hourly bars honest. Without it, a stay that straddles an hour line has to be filed somewhere whole — usually under the hour where it started or the hour where it ended — and whichever hour "wins" gets credit for time it never contained. A ninety-minute stay across noon is not a lunchtime event; it is thirty minutes of the eleven o'clock hour and sixty of the twelve. Splitting at the boundary gives each hour exactly its own seconds, no more and no less, so the bars still sum correctly when you roll them up into days.

This is not a simplified description — it is genuinely how the product works. Occupancy history in the console is the sum of per-space state intervals, split at hour boundaries, expressed as a share of the hour's total space-seconds. When the chart says the ten o'clock hour ran at 68 percent, it means 68 percent of the available space-time was actually consumed, not that 68 changes happened to be observed.

/04Good intervals need good edges

A time-weighted average is only as honest as the intervals beneath it, which puts real pressure on detection. Sightline analyzes the camera feed continuously, five frames every second, and a space changes state only after several consecutive frames agree — so a car driving past, or a shadow crossing the pavement, never opens a phantom interval. Systems that sample on an interval face a harder problem: between one snapshot and the next, the seconds are unrecorded and can only be assumed. Reading the lot continuously means each interval's edges are accurate to well under a second, and the hourly sums inherit that accuracy.

/05What changes when you measure time

The first thing operators notice in honest hourly history is that their peak-churn hour and their peak-occupancy hour are usually not the same hour. Turnover peaks when short visits stack up; fullness peaks when long stays overlap. Staffing, enforcement windows, and permit decisions each key off a different one of those, and an event-counted chart quietly hands you the wrong peak for two of the three. Time-weighted history separates them cleanly — and because it comes with the same flat software pricing as the live counts, it is not an analytics upsell; it is just how the history is computed.

Measured in events, a parking lot is a story about its most restless cars. Measured in seconds, it is a story about the asset itself — every space, every hour, credited with exactly the time it held.
