The economics of counting parking with cameras you already own

Monish Munagala 2026-08-28 6 min read

The most expensive part of knowing whether a parking space is taken has never been the knowing. Software that can look at a car and say car stopped being exotic years ago. The expensive part is the physics: getting a sensor to the space. Multiply that by a hundred spaces, in weather, in traffic, in pavement that will one day be repaved, and you have the real budget line — the one that has kept per-space parking data a luxury product for two decades.

It's worth walking through that arithmetic slowly, because it explains almost everything about how this industry is priced.

/01The arithmetic of hardware

The classic answer to occupancy is a device physically in the space: a sealed puck sitting in or on the pavement, one per space, reporting over a radio network. The engineering is genuinely good — the leading in-ground sensors are millimeters thin, rated for tons of load per wheel, with batteries specified up to ten years. But the unit economics are unforgiving. A 100-space lot needs one hundred sensors, plus the radio gateways that collect them. Each one is fixed to the pavement with adhesive or cored into it. Nothing about that scales gracefully: space 101 costs what space 1 cost.

Garage guidance systems soften the count but keep the construction. A camera unit mounted over every drive aisle watches up to six bays apiece — call it seventeen units for a hundred spaces — and the install threads ceiling channel, CAT6, and power runs through the whole structure. Overhead sensor systems compress the count further still, with a single mast-mounted device covering up to a hundred spaces, but the device is still proprietary, still purchased, and still needs a pole, a mount, and a power feed.

It is tempting to negotiate on unit price, but the unit price is the least durable number on the quote. The count is what endures. A hundred devices is a hundred purchase decisions, a hundred installation visits, and — years later, on a schedule the vendor sets — a hundred service calls. Whatever the shape of the hardware, every device count drives three budgets at once: the purchase, the installation, and the maintenance. The first is the only one that fits on a quote.

/02Installation is a construction project

The second budget arrives in high-visibility vests. Coring pavement means closing rows. Ceiling cabling means lifts in the aisles and conduit through the structure. Mast mounting means trenching power to wherever the pole stands. While all of it happens, the lot is partly a work site — which for a parking business means the asset is busy costing money precisely where it usually earns it.

And the third budget never really ends. A ten-year battery is an honest spec and also a promise: at some point, someone kneels at every single space with a tool. Repave the lot and the in-pavement install happens twice. Reconfigure the layout — angle the rows, add EV charging — and devices move with it. The device count you bought on day one follows you around for the life of the system.

Every approach except software has a device count attached to it, and the count is what drives cost for the life of the system.

/03The sensor you already bought

Here is the part that changes the arithmetic: most lots worth instrumenting are already inside a camera's field of view. Security cameras went up years ago, for entirely different reasons, and nearly every one made in the last decade speaks the same standard streaming protocol, RTSP. One fixed camera can hold a hundred spaces in frame — Lot A, the real lot this whole site is built on, is exactly that: one view, one hundred spaces.

Sightline's whole economic argument sits in that fact. The sensor network your lot needs is, in most cases, already bolted to your building and already depreciated on someone's books. What was missing was software that reads it per space, continuously, on your own hardware — so the marginal hardware cost of per-space data drops to zero, because the hardware exists. The "installation" is drawing each space's outline on the frame once. When you repave, you redraw polygons in an afternoon instead of re-coring a hundred holes.

Honesty requires the caveat: this only works where a camera can see. A curb with no coverage, an isolated corner, a low deck no lens reaches — there, a device in the space is the right answer, and we say so plainly in our comparison. The economics of cameras are the economics of coverage you already have.

/04What the software model changes

Once the sensor is software, cost stops scaling with spaces at all. There is no device to multiply by a hundred, so Sightline's pricing scales with sites instead — a flat monthly rate, whether the camera watches forty spaces or four hundred. Year one contains no construction project, and year eight contains no battery campaign. The failure modes change shape too: a software problem is fixed in an update pushed to your machine, not a truck roll to a device in the pavement.

The budgeting conversation changes with it. A hardware system is a capital project — quotes, approvals, closures, a depreciation schedule — and capital projects are exactly the meetings where per-space data has historically gone to die. A software subscription is an operating line that starts this month and can stop next month, which means the decision can be made by the person who actually runs the lot, on the evidence of their own footage.

The data gets better at the same time the cost gets simpler. A camera-based system reads the lot continuously — Sightline analyzes five frames every second — rather than sampling it on an interval, and the same feed that produces the counts shows you the lot itself, which no buried sensor can.

None of this required inventing new hardware. That is the quiet point of the whole argument: the cheapest sensor is the one already bolted to your building. The economics of parking data stopped being a hardware problem the day your cameras went up — the remaining step is a thirty-minute call where we point software at them and you watch your own lot become numbers.
